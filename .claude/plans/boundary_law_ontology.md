# What a geometry can promise at its boundary (living plan, opened 2026-09-30)

Status: **discussion**. Implementation starts only when the user rules this plan polished (plan-authoring §0). It blocks one decision of #405 P1: whether `StructuredGeometry` stores the same face-law value as the meshes (the question put at the close of P1 step 3c, `.claude/plans/reference_cache.md`, the ⏸ point of 2026-09-30).

## The goal, in the domain's terms

A geometry states a physical problem's domain. Some boundary statements are facts about that domain, and every method that solves the problem must honour them or refuse. Others are models of what lies outside the domain, and a method may honour one exactly, approximately (a declared approximation), or not at all. The goal: each kind of statement lives in the object whose promise it is, so that a method can never silently substitute a different problem, and so that the reference architecture (#405) can say which equation a solution answers.

## What the tree already holds `[M]` 2026-09-30

The corpus already has the two kinds (`docs/theory/foundations/boundary_conditions.rst`, sections `bc-factor-roles`, `bc-factor-quotients`, `bc-equivariance`, `bc-refusal-axes`):
- A law is the affine map `γ₋ψ = R G γ₊ψ + q`. `G` is the **deck transformation** of a quotient of the domain (the domain genuinely continues across the face: a mirror plane, a periodic wrap, a rotation). `R` is the **response kernel**, the constitutive law (vacuum, albedo, white/Lambertian, a specular wall). `q` is a prescribed inflow. Exactly one of `G`, `R` is non-trivial.
- Membership is decided by two tests: multiplicativity (necessary: an average is never `G`) and "is it a quotient of the domain?" (sufficient: a polished wall is a permutation but not a quotient, so its specular return is `R`).
- A `G` has a discrete realization exactly when the discretization is **equivariant** under the group element: a mirror needs a quadrature closed under the reflection (the checks behind ERR-042/044/045); a wrap acts only on space, so every angular discretization is trivially equivariant.
- Refusals lie on three independent axes: angular resolution (can the method represent `R`'s angular structure?), spatial/topological (can its operator couple two faces? diffusion refuses periodic), and the state cone (can the value be represented? SN refuses zero-flux, whose albedo is −1). `q` is a fourth thing, a vector on `Γ₋`.
- The laws live in `orpheus/geometry/boundary/`; the geometry and the meshes store them as tags or law objects, one per boundary point or face, with no distinction of kind.

## Observations raised for the discussion

1. **A deck law is not a boundary condition of the domain; it is the domain's topology.** Across a periodic pair nothing is a boundary (the quotient is a torus); a mirror plane is the reflector locus of an orbifold. So the geometry's honest content is "this domain is a fundamental domain of the group generated by these identifications", and its true boundary is only the response faces. `[R]`
2. **"Every method honours a deck law" holds with two decidable conditions, never as an approximation.** (a) The discretization is equivariant under the group (the quadrature closed under the mirror; for MoC, a track laydown commensurate with the face pairing; for a 2-D mesh, conforming cell faces on a periodic pair). (b) The realizer's codomain can hold the coupling (diffusion's periodic refusal is a per-face realizer shape, the topological axis, not a limit of P1 theory). Both are admission predicates: a deck law is honoured exactly or refused. `[R]`
3. **A response law is a model of the exterior, and its realization carries a status.** Exact (MC for any sampleable kernel; SN white up to the quadrature?), a named approximation (diffusion's vacuum is Marshak), or refused (on one of the three axes). It belongs to the problem's statement of the exterior, and a solution declares which realization it used (#405's "every solution declares the equation it answers"). `[R]`, to be measured per method × law.
4. **The tag `reflective` names two different objects, and the geometry decides which.** On a flat face (a slab) it is a mirror plane, a deck law. On a curved surface (a cylinder's or sphere's outer surface) no isometry of space mirrors across it, so it is a specular wall, a response. `[M]` 2026-09-30: `SNProblem` realizes `BC("reflective")` on a sphere's outer surface as `ReflectiveBoundary` with `geometry_map = SelfPairedDeck(mirror)`, the same object as on a slab face; by the corpus's own sufficient test it is `R` (`AlbedoBoundary(1, SpecularReturn)`). The matrix is the same in 1-D; the promise is not: a mirrored slab is a lattice (an infinite medium in x), a specular sphere is a closed mirrored cavity. The Wigner–Seitz cell's white law is the response that stands in for a lattice quotient the cylinder cannot carry.
5. **The per-face seed applies to responses only.** A deck law is a global isometry, so it covers a whole plane or a whole pair of faces; it can never vary along a surface. A response can (part of a surface faces a reflector, part faces vacuum). So the 2-D "several faces, several laws" seed is a statement about responses. `[R]`

## Second exchange (2026-09-30): the symmetry group first

**What that implies, for discussion** (each `[R]` until the census and the symmetry survey report):

1. **The geometry states a colouring of its boundary, not the responses.** The response classes partition the true boundary faces ("A and B obey the same response"; "A obeys X, B obeys Y"). The interior already carries a colouring of the same kind, the material ids: the geometry says which regions share a material, never what the material is. The responses' content is the exterior's, and each method realizes it with a status.
2. **The symmetry group is inferred, not declared.** The problem's symmetry group is the subgroup of the domain's isometries that preserves both colourings: materials inside, response classes on the boundary. A slab `[0, L]` of one material with both faces in one class has the mirror at `x = L/2`; with two classes it has none. The inference needs only equality of classes, which is exactly what the geometry knows.
3. **The coordinate system is already a quotient by the continuous part of that group.** A 1-D slab is 3-D space divided by translations in y and z; a 1-D cylinder divides by rotations about the axis and translations along it; a 1-D sphere divides by all rotations about the centre. The centre `r = 0` "carries no law" because it is the fixed-point set of that group, the singular locus of the quotient (a regularity condition, which is how the SN pole is already treated). `CoordSystem` is therefore a name for a symmetry group, and the corpus's rule "an orbit space is named by its stabiliser" applies to it.
4. **Deck faces are what the discrete part of the group leaves when the domain is folded.** A mirror symmetry, once quotiented out, becomes a mirror face; a lattice translation becomes a periodic pair. So a deck law is derived from the symmetry group and the choice to reduce the domain, not stated as input. The full domain with its colourings is the primary object; the reduced domain with deck faces is a derived one, and every method must give the same answer on both (a verification identity).
5. **The declared colouring is authoritative, never corrected.** `[REFUTED 2026-09-30 as a concern]` The first draft called the inferred group "a lower bound" to be guarded. The user's ruling: two classes declared different and later filled with the same response is the user's choice, possibly the very comparison being studied (both faces reflective against one reflective and one not). The code exploits the symmetry the user DECLARED and nothing more. When the exterior fills two classes with equal responses, the code may emit a diagnostic ("these two boundary classes carry the same law; the geometry admits a larger symmetry and could be simplified"), never a refusal. The purpose of the colouring is to let a geometry offer its advantages early, before the physics is fixed.
6. **"The same response" must be read in each face's own frame.** A response is a kernel on the face's inflow trace, written relative to the face's outward normal. An isometry mapping A to B carries A's frame to B's, and a mirror reverses the tangential orientation. For an isotropic, albedo or Lambertian response nothing changes; for a response that depends on direction or on position along the face (a prescribed inflow), the symmetry holds only if the response is equivariant. The colouring therefore needs either responses expressed in face-local frames, or a flag for the responses that are not invariant. Open.

**The candidate this suggests, O4** (it refines O2): the geometry owns the domain, the coordinate system (a named continuous group), the material colouring and the boundary colouring (response classes, keyed by face). From these it infers its symmetry group. Deck laws are not stored; they are produced by a fold (the quotient by a chosen subgroup), and the folded geometry records which faces are mirror or periodic faces. The exterior assigns a response to each class. A method needs a discretization equivariant under the group it is asked to use.

## Survey B's findings (2026-09-30; `scratch/boundary_ontology/survey_symmetry.md`, tree at `5a5ffa7b`)

`[M]` unless marked:
- **The group machinery is angular only.** `SubgroupOfO3`, the invariance closure, `Quotient` and the Reynolds lift all act on the sphere of directions; the orbit-space catalogue has 6 keys, all on the sphere. The element type `RigidMotion` reaches every rigid motion, translations included, but `close_group` refuses any infinite group: a translation, or two parallel mirrors (a slab mirrored at both faces generates the infinite dihedral group, which is the lattice).
- **`CoordSystem` and `AxisCoord` know no group.** Of 126 functions naming a group type and 108 naming a coordinate system, 0 name both. What is typed is the angular group each coordinate system integrates away, the point stabiliser, in `GEOMETRY_ANGULAR_SYMMETRY` (`numerics/quadrature/registry.py:1161`: `spent`, `unspent`, `owed`). It is keyed by `slab`/`sphere`/`cylinder`/`cartesian2d`, a vocabulary disjoint from `CoordSystem`'s, with no crosswalk, and no production code reads it (27 callers of `select_quadrature`, all tests).
- **The coordinate system's consequences are restated by hand.** 67 sites in 16 files branch on the coordinate system (55 outside `coord.py`, whose docstring calls itself the single point of coordinate dependence). "The centre carries no law" is stated (`coord.py:115`), not derived. SN's pole regularity is realized with `SelfPairedDeck.mirror("x")`, the type of a reflective face (`sn/loss_representation/__init__.py:4040`).
- **Deck types are elements, never groups.** `SelfPairedDeck`/`PairedDeck` are built from single `RigidMotion`s; no code forms the group a geometry's deck faces generate, and no code infers a symmetry: every spatial symmetry is declared as a law tag. A law checks the quadrature one motion at a time at realization; no realizer reads the registry's `owed` group.
- **The σ_x twin.** The mirror exists as a group (`SubgroupOfO3.Mirror("x")`) and as a motion (`SelfPairedDeck`), built from two letter tables each documented as the single source (`face_layout.py:100`, `symmetry.py:118`); no morphism relates them and no test compares them.
- **Folding.** No production code builds a reduced domain from a full one; 3 full-versus-folded tests exist, all on one Peierls derivation. (The SN cylinder's angular fold by σ_y is mandatory, and is the one fold that exists.)

**What this says about the connection** `[R]`:
- The phase space is space × directions, and a symmetry acts on both. A coordinate system names a continuous spatial group `G_c`: translations in y and z (slab), rotations about an axis with translations along it (cylinder), rotations about a point (sphere). The 1-D coordinate is the orbit space `R³/G_c`.
- At a point of that orbit space, the isotropy subgroup (the elements of `G_c` fixing the point) acts on the directions. That isotropy group is exactly `GEOMETRY_ANGULAR_SYMMETRY`'s `spent`: O(2) about the normal for a slab, about the radius for a sphere, hence the angular flux depends on μ alone in both. So the tree types the second half of the chain (the angular stabiliser) and not the first (the spatial group it is the stabiliser of), and it keys the second half by strings disconnected from `CoordSystem`.
- Where the isotropy jumps (the sphere's centre, where it is all of O(3); the cylinder's axis) the orbit space has a singular point. There the equation needs a regularity condition, not a law, which is why the centre carries no law and why the pole is treated as a closure. Today both facts are stated by hand, and the pole reuses the reflective face's type.
- The discrete part of the problem's group (mirrors, lattice translations: in 2-D, a wallpaper group such as p4m for a square lattice) is what O4 infers from the colourings. Representing it needs a spatial group type that can hold infinite discrete groups by their generators and a fundamental domain, which the machinery refuses today.

**The reflective boundary as a deck transformation** `[R]`, from the corpus and `SelfPairedDeck`:
- It is a statement about the manifold: the face is a symmetry plane of the problem, the domain is a fundamental domain of the group the mirror generates, and the solution on the other side is the mirror image of the solution on this side. `G` is the Koopman operator of the mirror `Q = I − 2 n̂ n̂ᵀ`, `R = I`, `q = 0`.
- Its only datum is which plane. It has no amplitude, because a symmetry statement adds no physics: "a symmetry plane cannot absorb". An albedo on it is a category error, not a parameter.
- On a self-paired face the mirror acts on directions only (`SelfPairedDeck` refuses an affine part: the face maps to itself, so the plane's offset is invisible to every discrete quantity). The plane is located by the face it is declared on.
- It exists only where space has that isometry: a flat face whose plane is a symmetry of the coordinate system's group. On a cylinder's or sphere's outer surface there is no such mirror of space, so "reflective" there is a specular wall, `AlbedoBoundary(1, SpecularReturn)`, a response. Today both are the same `ReflectiveBoundary` object (Census A, section 3).
- Group consequences: one mirrored face of a slab doubles the domain (`Z₂`); both faces mirrored generate the infinite dihedral group, whose orbit is the infinite lattice (why `close_group` refuses it, survey B).

## Fifth exchange (2026-10-01): two families, and a queryable admission

**The user:** split into two groups, perhaps with a parent class: deck transformations (`MirrorDeck` or a better name, `RotationDeck`, `TranslationDeck`) and boundary conditions. Deck transformations would be guaranteed in every method and used to compute the symmetry group (and the centre of mass); boundary conditions are resolved per method. The place a boundary condition is picked should be queryable: a front end selects a method and offers a drop-down of the conditions known to exist for it, to attach onto a declared boundary.

**Reading, for discussion** `[R]`:
1. **A deck law IS its deck element.** `ReflectiveBoundary` and `PeriodicBoundary` are wrappers whose whole content is `G` (`R = I`, `q = 0`): the law object adds nothing but the wrong slot (the albedo). So the split retires the wrapper rather than adding a family beside it: the deck family is the face pairing itself, a motion plus the faces it pairs. The concept count goes down (the law and its `G` were two objects for one thing).
2. **The motion decides the pairing.** A face lying in a mirror plane is paired with itself (`SelfPairedDeck`, the crossing in angle); a translation, a rotation and a glide reflection pair it with a different face (`PairedDeck`, the crossing in space). The identity is the trivial pairing every response carries, and it is not a deck at all once responses stop carrying a `G`. So naming by motion (mirror, translation, rotation) is consistent with the arithmetic, and the self-/distinct-pairing split becomes derived, a property of the motion, not a type axis.
3. **The two families live in different places.** Deck transformations belong to the geometry: they say the domain is a fundamental domain of a group (or are produced by folding a full domain by its declared symmetry, the fourth exchange's O4). The group they generate, with the coordinate system's continuous group, gives the fixed-point sets (the centre, the axis), which is where "the centre carries no law" and the pole regularity come from. Boundary conditions belong to the exterior: they are attached to the geometry's boundary classes (its colouring) and realized per method.
4. **"Guaranteed in every method" becomes a contract with a gate**, not an assumption: every method × every deck in its coordinate system is realized exactly, or its absence is a recorded gap of that method. `[M]` Census A: of 5 deck refusals today, 4 are missing registry entries and 1 is structural (diffusion's per-face boundary operator cannot couple a periodic pair). The equivariance condition (a quadrature closed under the mirror, tracks closing on a pair) is checked when the problem is posed.
5. **The queryable place exists in a weak form: each method's `BOUNDARY_OPERATOR_REGISTRY`**, a tag → law-class table (`[M]` SN: vacuum, reflective; diffusion: vacuum, reflective, albedo, zero_flux). It is not the truth today: a typed law bypasses it and is refused (or, before ERR-094, mis-realized) only at realization. The proposal: one admission query per method, keyed by face, answering for each condition whether the method realizes it exactly, as a named approximation, or not (with the axis), from the same place the realizer reads. The front end's drop-down is that query; the tag table and the realizer's refusals become its two consumers.
6. **A shared parent?** Both close the transport equation at a face, and the realizer consumes both. A protocol for what the realizer reads (the affine trace map) is justified; a shared base class carrying implementation is not, since the two families are owned by different objects and refused for different reasons.

## Sixth exchange (2026-10-01): is this the orbifold?

**The user:** asks whether this arrangement is the best way to implement the orbifold. On the centre of mass: in an earlier (CSG) discussion, the way to derive a geometry's point symmetry was to compute its centre of mass, the point about which point-symmetry operations are checked, possibly by Monte Carlo sampling of the geometry.

**Ruled (the user, 2026-10-01):**
- The deck family: `DeckTransformation` with `MirrorDeck`, `TranslationDeck`, `RotationDeck`. The response family: `BoundaryCondition`; the member names (`AlbedoBoundary` vs `AlbedoResponse` vs another) are open to suggestion.
- Scope: decks now, responses later.
- Decks are declared on the geometry (otherwise the point symmetry group could never be derived from it), at the place the coordinate system is decided: both are topology.

**The orbifold, as the tree would need it** `[R]`:
- An orbifold is a quotient `X / Γ` of a space by a group acting properly. Two equivalent presentations: the group acting on the full space (the full domain and its symmetries; folding produces the rest), or a fundamental domain with face pairings (Poincaré's polyhedron theorem: the pairings generate Γ, subject to the cycle condition at every edge cycle, where the composed pairings must be a rotation by 2π/k).
- Beyond the face pairings it has a **singular locus**: the points with non-trivial isotropy, each carrying its isotropy group. Mirror faces (isotropy Z₂), cone points (a rotation centre, Z_k), corner reflectors where mirrors meet (dihedral D_k), and, from the continuous part, the sphere's centre (isotropy O(3)) and the cylinder's axis (O(2)). At a singular point the transport equation takes a regularity condition, not a law: the existing pole treatment, and "the centre carries no law".
- So decks-on-faces is the right generator presentation, but not the whole object. The object is `(the continuous group the coordinate system names, the deck generators, the fundamental domain)`. From it derive: the discrete group Γ, the singular strata with their isotropy groups, and the faces that remain true boundary, which take boundary conditions. In 1-D every piece is small (one mirror: Z₂; two mirrors: the infinite dihedral group, the lattice; a translation pair: Z), but it is the same shape as the 2-D and 3-D case.
- **The centre of mass, generalised.** A finite symmetry group of a bounded body fixes its centroid (the centroid is equivariant under any isometry preserving the body and its material colouring), so the centroid is the only candidate centre of every point operation, and the inertia tensor's principal axes are the candidate axes (a degenerate eigenvalue pair signals a rotation axis of order ≥ 3). Monte Carlo moments estimate both for a CSG body. An infinite discrete group (a lattice) fixes no point; there each point of special isotropy (a Wyckoff position) is found per stabiliser. This is the full-domain route (derive Γ, then fold); declared decks are the fundamental-domain route; both build the same orbifold value.
- **The missing machinery** (survey B): a spatial group held by generators, not by enumeration (`close_group` refuses every infinite group today), and the link from a coordinate system to the continuous group it names.

**Naming the responses** `[R]`: "Response" is taken by the kernel tier (`ScalarResponse`, `LambertianReemission`, `SpecularReemission` are the `R` factors), so `AlbedoResponse` would put a law and a kernel under one token. Following the family's own name keeps the token unique: `VacuumCondition`, `AlbedoCondition`, `WhiteCondition`, `InflowCondition`, `ZeroFluxCondition` under `BoundaryCondition`, as the decks are `*Deck` under `DeckTransformation`. One collision to settle when the responses are done: the tag class `BC` abbreviates "boundary condition".
